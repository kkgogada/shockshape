    Without a thickness or area constraint the drag minimum is a zero-thickness flat plate. With one, the optimizer has to trade shape against shock strength, which is real airfoil design.

    How it works

    One interface, three physics models

    Every model answers the same question: shape, Mach, α → surface Cp, CL, CD. The editor and the optimizer never know which one is running.

    Shock-expansion theory (M ≳ 1.3): an oblique shock or Prandtl–Meyer expansion at the leading edge, then isentropic simple waves along each surface. For thin, sharp sections with an attached shock this is the exact inviscid surface solution apart from weak reflected waves. Linearized (Ackeret) theory is drawn alongside so you can see where it breaks down.

    Transonic small-disturbance solver (M ≈ 0.5–1.3): the nonlinear TSD equation on a stretched 2-D grid, discretized with Murman’s conservative type-dependent scheme and solved by line relaxation with grid sequencing and Anderson acceleration. It captures embedded shocks, the sonic line, and shock wave drag (Oswatitsch entropy formula).

    Neural surrogate: a small tanh MLP trained offline on a sweep of flow solutions. It evaluates in microseconds and its gradients come from one backward pass.

    The design loop

    The optimizer minimises CD subject to a lift target (α is a design variable) and a thickness or area floor, using an augmented Lagrangian with BFGS steps.

    The gradient is the expensive part. With finite differences every design iteration costs one flow solve per design variable (11 here). That is why real shape optimization, including the SU2 work this app extends, uses adjoint methods: the full gradient for roughly the cost of one extra solve, however many design variables there are.

    The neural surrogate plays the adjoint’s role in the browser: its back-propagated gradient costs one pass regardless of dimension. The eval counter under the convergence plot makes the difference visible.

    Try it: at Mach 2 with a fixed t/c the optimizer heads toward a double-wedge; with a fixed area it heads toward a biconvex. That is exactly what linearized supersonic theory predicts.

    Validation

    Automated tests (npm test) check the engines against closed-form results:

    • Prandtl–Meyer, oblique-shock and Cp* values against NACA 1135 tables
    • Flat plate shock-expansion lift computed by hand; CD = CL tan α
    • Biconvex wave drag → Ackeret’s 16t²/(3β) as t → 0
    • TSD lift and thickness pressures vs. Prandtl–Glauert theory (≤ 2%)
    • Shock-free subsonic flow: zero wave drag (d’Alembert)
    • TSD at M = 1.5 vs. Ackeret and shock-expansion (≤ 3–5%)
    • Surrogate back-prop gradients vs. finite differences; surrogate-optimized designs re-checked with the true model

    Spot checks against SU2 Euler runs from the offline pipeline are listed in the README.

    Limits, stated plainly: inviscid models only, so there is no skin friction and no separation. TSD assumes thin airfoils and over-predicts shock strength compared with Euler. Shock-expansion needs an attached leading-edge shock (the app tells you when it detaches). The bundled surrogate is a placeholder trained on shock-expansion data (M 1.4–3) so the whole chain runs end to end; the pipeline in pipeline/ regenerates it from SU2 Euler runs.
